## 3 How it works `/methodology/`

Title: How I work with founders | PainToBuild

Description: I start with your decision, review what you know and compare the options. I suggest a test, then review what happened and what you might do next.

# How I work with you

I start by understanding the decision you need to make. The research and review then focus on what would help you make that decision.

## Understand the question

I ask what you are deciding, why it matters now and what you have tried. I also ask about your time and budget. Each review covers one decision so the work stays manageable.

## Review the information {#evidence-categories}

I may review your product, customer comments, usage numbers or past efforts to find customers. The agreed work may also include looking at other products and public discussions.

I make clear which details you supplied, which I checked and which are my interpretation.

## Consider other explanations

There may be several reasons something is not working. I look for information that supports each explanation and information that goes against it. I also consider whether the product solves a problem people care enough about.

## Choose a test {#decision-criteria}

I recommend a test that fits your time and budget. The review explains what to do, what to watch for and how the result could change the advice.

If more research is needed first, I explain what to find out.

## Review the results

After you run the test, I look at what happened. I check whether the result answers the original question and explain what I would do next.

A test that was not run tells you something different from a test that people rejected. Missing or unreliable numbers may mean the result is still unclear.

## How I use public research {#independence}

Public posts can point to problems worth looking into. I avoid counting the same person's story several times. Different accounts may belong to one person, and people may repeat something they read elsewhere. When that is unclear, I note it.

Several people describing a problem can be a reason to investigate. It does not prove that your product will sell.

### Which reports are useful? {#strength-levels}

A detailed account can tell you what happened, what the person tried and why it mattered. A short expression of interest usually tells you less.

The important question is whether the report helps answer the question you are researching.

### What the research shows {#confidence-tiers}

I separate what people reported from possible explanations and unanswered questions. I use counts only when I can explain what was counted and show the records behind them.

### What people already use {#contradictions}

People may already solve the problem with another product, a spreadsheet or help from someone else. They may also choose to live with it.

I look at these options before suggesting that a new product is needed. That includes checking whether an ordinary AI tool can do the job well enough.

### What payment claims mean {#external-validation}

A price on a website shows that something is for sale. It does not show that anyone bought it.

Someone saying they might pay shows interest. A report of a purchase tells you more, but it is still a report unless checked.

A confirmed payment shows that one purchase happened. It does not prove that other people will buy your offer.

### What I leave out of the count {#exclusions}

I do not count repeated posts, general advice or sales claims as separate reports of customer need.

A comment can still be useful if the writer describes their own problem. It does not have to be the first post in a discussion.

### A problem is not proof of a sale {#problem-vs-payment}

You still need to find out who can buy, how to reach them and why they would choose your product. You also need to understand the cost of finding and serving those customers.

### What the research cannot tell you {#limitations}

People who post online do not represent everyone. Their accounts may leave out important details. Information about competing products can go out of date. Small tests can also give misleading results.

Some older guides use a worksheet that scores ideas in twelve areas. It helps organize the research. The score does not decide whether a business will work or what advice I give a client.

### Sources and private information {#privacy}

Research articles include relevant links and dates. A source link may reveal the writer's name even if it is not shown on my page.

Before you share private business information, I explain how it will be handled. The privacy page describes those arrangements.

## 5 Validation article `/evidence/reddit-product-validation/`

Title: What Reddit posts can tell you about an idea | PainToBuild

Description: Public posts can help you understand a problem. Learn what to check next before assuming people will try your product or pay for it.

# What can Reddit posts tell you about an idea?

This article is for you if you are looking into an idea before building it.

## What you can learn

Posts can show how people describe a problem and what they have tried. They may help you find questions to ask possible customers.

They cannot tell you, on their own, whether people will buy your product.

## Look at the problem being described

Note who has the problem, when it happens and why it matters. Look for what the person already does about it.

A founder asking for feedback on an idea is not the same as a customer asking for help with a problem. A founder asking “Would you pay this price?” has asked a question. It does not mean anyone has agreed to pay.

## Work out what you still need to know

| Question | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Who has this problem? | Recent examples from the people you want to help |
| What do they already use? | What works, what does not and why |
| Can you reach the person who buys? | A way to speak with someone who needs it and can approve the purchase |
| Will they try your approach? | A small test that asks them to do something relevant |
| Will they pay? | A clear offer and their response, followed by an actual purchase where appropriate |

## Choose a next step

If the problem is unclear, ask possible customers about recent examples. If you understand the problem but not the solution, try a simple model or do part of the work by hand.

If you need to learn whether people will pay, explain exactly what you are offering before asking them to buy. Make the price and terms clear.

Decide what result would change your mind. No fixed number of posts or interviews proves that an idea is ready to become a business.

## Keep the limits in mind

Someone who complains online may not be reachable or willing to buy. A person who tries something once may not use it again. A paid product in the market does not show that people will switch to yours.

These are separate questions to investigate.

## 6 Channel article `/evidence/community-access-is-not-buyer-access/`

Title: Are your possible customers in this community? | PainToBuild

Description: Before spending more time in an online community, check who takes part, whether they need your product and what you can learn from a small test.

# Are your possible customers in this community?

You may be posting regularly without knowing whether you are reaching people who could use your product.

## Look at who is there

Being able to post in a community does not mean its members are likely to buy from you. Look at what they do, what they need and whether your product fits.

Other founders may be customers too. It depends on what you sell.

## Ask these questions

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Do members have the problem you solve? | Their interest in the topic may not mean they need your product |
| Can they decide to buy it? | The person using a product may not control the budget |
| What do they use now? | Their current choice gives you something to compare with |
| Can you take part under the community's rules? | You need a suitable way to talk with people |
| What happens after someone shows interest? | A conversation, trial or purchase tells you more than a like |

## Consider other reasons for a poor response

You may be reaching the wrong people. Your explanation may be unclear. People may not trust the offer or feel a need to act now. The product itself may not be useful enough.

The amount of time you spend posting does not tell you which reason applies.

## Try a small test

Choose one type of customer and one problem they need to solve. Find a community where people discuss that problem. Follow its rules and take part in a useful way.

Decide what you want to learn. Record relevant conversations, product trials or purchases, depending on the test. Likes alone will not answer whether people need the product.

If little happens, check whether you reached the intended people before ruling out the whole community.

## What remains uncertain

Finding possible customers does not mean you can reach enough of them at a reasonable cost. People who need the product may not have the budget to buy it.

One founder's success with replies does not mean the same approach will work for you.

## 7 Product use article `/diagnostics/signups-no-core-feature-use/`

Title: Why people sign up but do not use your product | PainToBuild

Description: Look at where people stop after signing up. Check what they expected, what they tried and whether the product helped before choosing a change.

# Why do people sign up but not use your product?

People have registered, but few seem to get much further. Before changing the product or doing more marketing, look at what is happening.

## Check what the numbers show

First, make sure your records capture the action you care about. Have people had a real chance to use the product? Are you measuring the step that gives them its main benefit?

For an expense sharing app, creating an account is different from recording an expense and sharing it with someone.

## Look at each step separately

Finding the product, trying it, getting a useful result, returning and paying are different steps. A problem at one step does not explain every other step.

| What you notice | What to check |
|---|---|
| No useful actions are recorded | Whether the records work and whether people had a reason to use the product |
| People stop during setup | A confusing step, a technical problem or too much information to enter |
| People expected something else | What they understood from the page or message that brought them there |
| Some people use it while others leave | Differences in their needs and in how they found the product |
| People try it but do not return | Whether the need came up again and whether they chose another solution |

## Speak with people who stopped

Ask what they wanted to do, what happened and what they did next. Find out whether they still needed help with the task.

When possible, watch someone try the task. What they do may reveal something they did not mention in an interview.

Use what you learn to choose one change to test. Decide how long to watch and what result would make the change worth keeping.

## Avoid drawing a conclusion too soon

A few conversations can suggest an explanation. They do not prove it caused the problem.

Simpler setup will not fix a product people do not need. More visitors may lead to more accounts without more use. Someone trying it once does not show they will return.

# What a Founder Decision Review looks like

**This example is made up.** It shows the kind of review I would write. It is not a client project, and no results have been observed.

## The decision

A founder has made an app for sharing expenses. They want to decide whether to add features or improve how they explain the app to possible users.

## What I am assuming for this example

The person organizing the expenses needs the app. They can share a link that lets everyone else view the breakdown without installing it.

The founder has made demos but has not shown this benefit clearly. There is no reliable information about whether people return to the app or would pay for it.

## Possible reasons for the problem

People may not understand what the app helps them do. The demos may be reaching people who do not organize shared expenses. Entering the information may take too much effort.

It is also possible that a spreadsheet or message already does the job well enough.

## What I would try first

I would ask a small number of willing organizers to use the current app for a real shared expense. I would explain the link sharing feature and watch where they need help or stop.

This would help me decide whether the first problem to address is the explanation, the steps in the app or the benefit itself.

## What I would look for

Can the organizer enter the expense and share it? Can the recipients view the breakdown? What help does anyone need?

When another shared expense comes up, does the organizer choose the app again? If no new expense occurs, that does not tell me whether they would return.

## How the result could change my advice

If people cannot finish the task, I would look into the step that blocks them.

If they finish but prefer their old method, I would ask why the app is not worth using again.

If they use it again without help, I would check whether more people have the same experience before making a broader claim.

## The later review

After the test, I would compare what happened with the possible reasons listed above. I would then explain what I recommend checking or changing next.

A thank you or a demo view would not show that someone had adopted the app.
